## Confirmed problems (8)

### 1. Byng’s Third Army given a 64-kilometre front of twelve divisions

Site (`operation_michael`, secondary, what): “The attack hit Byng’s Third Army on a 64-kilometre front held by twelve divisions.”

- Ian Beckett, Timothy Bowman and Mark Connelly, *The British Army and the First World War* (Cambridge University Press, 2017), chapter 10: “while Byng was holding twenty-eight miles with fourteen infantry divisions, Gough was holding forty-two miles with just twelve divisions.”
- L. C. F. Turner, introduction to Volume V of the Australian official history (Australian War Memorial): the Third Army under Byng held a forty-five kilometre front, and the Fifth Army under Gough was stretched along sixty-seven kilometres; seventy-six German divisions were hurled against fourteen divisions of the Third Army and twelve of the Fifth.

### 2. Sandfontein: strengths and losses given to the German side

Site (`battle_of_tanga`, note): “the battle of Sandfontein there, 26 September 1914, left 237 Germans with two guns holding off 1,700 men, with German losses of 16 killed, 51 wounded and 205 captured.”

- Imperial Research, “The Battle of Sandfontein”: the South African force surrendered and its prisoners were marched off; sixteen of that force were killed; the German force was given as about 1,700 rifles, and the German casualty list as about sixty, including fourteen dead.
- South African Military History Society, journal account drawn from Neil Orpen: Colonel Grant’s South African force had sixteen killed and was captured, apart from a few who escaped; the German force was given as about 1,500 rifles with about sixty casualties.

### 3. The Senate’s war vote dated 2 April

Site (`united_states_declares_war`, note): “The votes were 82 to 6 in the Senate on 2 April and 373 to 50 in the House on 6 April.” The secondary text says Wilson asked for war on 2 April, “which the Senate passed that day by 82 to 6.”

The request on 2 April, and the House vote on 6 April, are not this item.

- United States Senate Historical Office, on the declaration of war with Germany: the Senate approved the resolution by 82 to 6 on 4 April 1917.
- Leonard Schlup, “North Dakota Senator Asle J. Gronna and the Isolationists, 1915–1920” (*North Dakota History*): the war resolution passed the Senate on 4 April by 82 to 6, and the House on 6 April by 373 to 50.

### 4. Erzurum said to have fallen in March

Site (`caucasus_1916`, summary): “Erzurum, the fortress that covered the road into eastern Anatolia, fell in March.”

The event card `erzurum_falls` dates the surrender to 16 February 1916. This item is the campaign sentence.

- firstworldwar.com, Morgan Price’s report, headed “The Russian Capture of Erzerum, 16 February 1916.”
- thegreatwar.pl, “Russians capture Erzurum (16 February 1916)”: the date is given by the British chronology of the war and by W. E. D. Allen and Paul Muratoff. The Julian date in Russian use was 3 February, which is also not March.

### 5. The 25 April landings split as two beaches at Helles and three at Anzac

Site (`gallipoli_1915`, summary): “The landings were made at five beaches on the morning of 25 April 1915, two at Cape Helles at the foot of the peninsula and three round Gaba Tepe and Anzac Cove further up the west coast.”

- National Army Museum, “Gallipoli campaign”: the 29th Division landed on five beaches at Helles, and a second landing was made by the Anzacs at Gaba Tepe, later Anzac Cove.
- Imperial War Museums, “The Gallipoli Campaign”: Ian Hamilton made two landings, the 29th Division at Cape Helles, where S, X and Y beaches are named, and the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps north of Gaba Tepe.

### 6. The last troops at Helles dated to the night of 7–8 January

Site (`gallipoli_1915`, summary): “those from Helles in the night of 7-8 January 1916.”

The event card `gallipoli_evacuation` says the final lift was completed on 8–9 January 1916. This item is the campaign sentence.

- Gallipoli Association, 9 January: the last stages at W Beach were early on 9 January 1916, and Lieutenant Langton-Jones was taken off at about 04:30.
- The Royal Scots, “Territorials/Dardanelles”: the 7th Battalion was the last element of the 52nd (Lowland) Division to leave, at 3 a.m. on 9 January 1916.

### 7. Chaytor’s Force, the Seventh Army, and Feisal at Amman

Site (`battle_of_megiddo`, secondary, what): “Chaytor’s force, Australian light horse and Arab irregulars, crossed the Jordan to pin the Seventh Army, and took Es Salt and Amman.”

Site (`arab_revolt_1916_18`, summary): “Amman was carried on 25 September 1918 by a force including the Arab troops of Feisal.”

- Australian War Memorial, caption B00093: Chaytor’s Force was the Anzac Mounted Division, the 20th Indian Infantry Brigade, two Jewish battalions and two British West Indies battalions; after the Seventh and Eighth Armies had been overthrown, Chaytor pursued the Fourth Army across the Jordan, and Amman was assailed on 25 September.
- NZ History, Auckland Mounted Rifles timeline for September 1918: Chaytor Force was the Anzac Mounted Division, the 20th Indian Brigade, the British West Indies Regiment and two battalions of the Royal Fusiliers; Amman was taken on 25 September by the New Zealand Mounted Rifles and the 2nd Australian Light Horse Brigade. Arab irregulars appear in that timeline on 29 September at Ziza, around a separate Ottoman column.

### 8. Hiiumaa taken on 13 October

Site (`operation_albion`, secondary, what): “Hiiumaa, attempted twice before, was taken on 13 October.”

- Eesti Entsüklopeedia, “Lääne-Eesti saarestiku lahingud 1917”: the Germans took Saaremaa on 16 October, Muhu on 18 October and Hiiumaa on 20 October.
- thegreatwar.pl, “Germans take Muhu and Hiiumaa” and “End of Operation Albion”: landings on Hiiumaa on 15, 16 and 17 October were turned back; the main landing was on 18 October; German studies put the last surrender on the evening of 19 October, and Russian sources and the British chronology put the occupation of the island on 20 October.

## Internal contradictions (5)

These are disagreements inside the snapshot. They are not offered as a corrected outside fact.

1. **Alekseev’s name.** The figure record is “Mikhail Vasilyevich Alekseev | 1857-1918”. The university biography says: “the roster calls him Nikolai Vasilievich Alekseev (1856-1918)”.

2. **“Eleven days” against the card’s own dates.** The Faustschlag title is “Eleven days: the German drive into the Baltic and Ukraine”, and the secondary text calls it “the Eleven Days’ War”. The note dates it “18 February – 3 March 1918”. That printed window is not an eleven-day span.

3. **“Six months of talks” against the snapshot’s own dates.** The Brest-Litovsk primary cause says “Six months of talks at Brest-Litovsk”. The armistice card says “Talks opened on 22 December”, and the treaty card is dated 3 March 1918.

4. **Two prisoner totals on Vittorio Veneto.** The card states “Some 448,000 soldiers and over 5,000 guns were taken in ten days”, and the note calls 448,000 about a third of the imperial and royal army. The same note records the article’s other series: 50,000 by 31 October, 100,000 by 1 November and 300,000 by 4 November, and says that series conflicts with the 448,000.

5. **Two defender strengths at Baku.** The primary text says the city was held by “some 20,000 ill-equipped troops”. The secondary text says “about 6,000 Centrocaspian troops” stood against Nuri Pasha. The note attributes 20,000 to the article’s infobox for the Baku Army.

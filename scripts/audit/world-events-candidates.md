# World-event candidates, July 1914 – November 1918

Checked against the live dump of 102 events (first `1914-07-23` `july_ultimatum_serbia`, last `1918-11-11` `armistice_of_11_november_1918`). The site’s month range is July 1914 through November 1918 (`TIMELINE_END`). Nothing here is dated after November 1918. Peace-conference, Versailles, Rowlatt and Amritsar events are out of range.

Counts: **23 ready**, **3 unconfirmed**, **already covered** as listed in the third section. No coordinates, strengths or casualty figures are proposed. Where a day or a label is disputed, the row says so.

A ready row has two publishers that are not the same house. Every Wikipedia language edition is one family, and is used only where a second house also asserts the fact. “Already covered” means the dump has a card whose subject is that event, or whose reading text narrates it. A fact that survives only in an editor’s `note` is treated as missing.

### `japan_declares_war_on_germany`

- **Date:** 1914-08-23
- **Theatre:** East Asia, politics
- **What:** Japan declared war on Germany, after an ultimatum demanding that Germany withdraw its ships from East Asian waters and give up Qingdao.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No card. The 1914 file header says Japan’s entry has no theatre of its own, and the reading text of `falklands_cruiser_war` does not narrate the declaration.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, World War I chronology: 23 August, “Japan declares war on Germany”.
  - National Army Museum, London, “Japan declares war on Germany”: 23 August 1914. Its siege article adds that the ultimatum was issued on 15 August and was refused.

### `togoland_surrenders`

- **Date:** 1914-08-26
- **Theatre:** Africa (Togoland)
- **What:** British and French colonial troops accepted the German surrender of Togoland after the Germans had destroyed the Kamina wireless station, the first German colony to fall.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No African card before `battle_of_tanga` (3 November). Togoland is not named in the 1914 reading text.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “History of Togo”: on 7 August British and French troops invaded, and on 26 August they “secured the unconditional surrender of the Germans”.
  - F. J. Moberly, *Military Operations, Togoland and the Cameroons* (official history; Imperial War Museum Department of Printed Books reprint): a letter of unconditional surrender was received on the morning of 26 August, and the ceremony was arranged for the following day.
- **Hedge:** Britannica’s own war chronology dates the Allied capture to 27 August. Use 26 August for the surrender and say that the entry into Kamina is also dated the 27th.

### `occupation_of_german_samoa`

- **Date:** 1914-08-29
- **Theatre:** Pacific
- **What:** A New Zealand expedition landed unopposed at Apia and occupied German Samoa, whose wireless station could reach the German squadron in the Pacific.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No Pacific card. Samoa is not named in the dump.
- **Publishers:**
  - NZ History (Manatū Taonga): the Samoa Advance Party landed at Apia on 29 August 1914 with no resistance; Colonel Logan proclaimed the occupation the next day.
  - Te Ara, *An Encyclopaedia of New Zealand* (1966): the force “landed unopposed at Apia” on 29 August.
- **Hedge:** The landing is 29 August; the proclamation read over the courthouse is 30 August (NZ History, and the National Museum of the Royal New Zealand Navy).

### `german_new_guinea_capitulates`

- **Date:** 1914-09-17
- **Theatre:** Pacific
- **What:** At Herbertshöhe, Acting Governor Eduard Haber gave Colonel William Holmes an assurance that military resistance in German New Guinea would cease; the armed forces were to be given up on 21 September.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No Pacific card. The Australian landing at Rabaul and the capitulation are not in the dump.
- **Publishers:**
  - Australian War Memorial, “Capture of German New Guinea”, and the terms transcribed in its Bean papers: the instrument is dated 17 September 1914 and says resistance shall cease at once, with the armed forces to surrender at Herbertshöhe on 21 September. The exhibition page says six Australians were killed in the brief resistance on landing.
  - Naval Historical Society of Australia, “The RAN and the Capitulation of German New Guinea”: Haber accepted on 17 September; the ceremony was on 21 September.
- **Hedge:** Do not call it an unconditional surrender. The instrument itself says Haber had no authority to surrender the possession and instead assured that resistance would cease.

### `duala_surrenders`

- **Date:** 1914-09-27
- **Theatre:** Africa (Kamerun)
- **What:** Duala, the port of German Kamerun, surrendered to an Anglo-French force under Charles Dobell after a naval bombardment, and the German field force fell back inland.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** Kamerun is not named in any card. The campaign’s start and its end (`mora_surrenders`, below) are both missing.
- **Publishers:**
  - Brigadier-General C. M. Dobell’s despatch, printed in the London Gazette of 31 May 1916 and reproduced by The Long, Long Trail: “the capitulation of Duala, 27th September, 1914”, after a bombardment on the 26th.
  - *The Spectator*, 31 October 1914, reporting a Press Bureau review: Duala “surrendered unconditionally” on 27 September to an Anglo-French force under Brigadier-General C. M. Dobell.
- **Hedge:** “Unconditionally” is the Press Bureau wording reported by *The Spectator*. Dobell’s own despatch says the towns of Duala and Bonaberi were surrendered. Do not add a prisoner count; Dobell’s “over 400 German Europeans” is one despatch, not a second independent tally.

### `fao_landing_mesopotamia`

- **Date:** 1914-11-06
- **Theatre:** Mesopotamia
- **What:** British and Indian troops of Indian Expeditionary Force D landed at Fao, at the mouth of the Shatt al-Arab, and opened the Mesopotamian campaign; Basra was occupied before the end of November.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `siege_of_kut` and `baghdad_falls` start the story in 1916–17. The 1914 landing is not a card. November 1914’s only extra-European card is `battle_of_tanga`.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum, “British-Indian troops land at Fao”: 6 November 1914. Its “Battle of Basra” entry dates the fighting that followed to 11–21 November and calls the result a British-Indian victory.
  - The Long, Long Trail, drawing on the British official history: on 6 November troops landed near the fort at Fao and captured it; Basra was entered on the evening of 21 November and officially taken into possession on 23 November.
- **Hedge:** Do not pin Basra’s entry to one day. The Long, Long Trail gives the evening of 21 November for the entry and 23 November for the formal occupation. Other accounts put the entry on 22 November. The landing at Fao on 6 November is the date both publishers share.

### `tsingtao_surrenders`

- **Date:** 1914-11 (day not confirmed by a second house)
- **Theatre:** East Asia
- **What:** The German and Austro-Hungarian garrison of Qingdao (Tsingtao) surrendered to a Japanese army with a small British contingent, ending the only major land campaign of the war in East Asia.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** It is one sentence in the `note` of `falklands_cruiser_war` (“the fall of Tsingtao on 7 November, after a siege begun on 27 August”), not in the reading text. The 1914 file header says Tsingtao has no theatre of its own in the `Front` list.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum, “Siege of Tsingtao”: the garrison surrendered on 7 November 1914 and handed over the port three days later. British losses in that article are 12 killed and 53 wounded; do not generalise them to the whole siege.
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Qingdao”: the port “capitulated after a blockade in November”. It does not give the day.
- **Hedge:** The November capitulation is the claim two houses share. The day 7 November is the National Army Museum plus English Wikipedia, which is one family and does not count as the second house. Do not put 7 November on a card until a second non-Wikipedia publisher gives that day. Leave casualty figures out.

### `suez_canal_raid`

- **Date:** 1915-02-03
- **Theatre:** Ottoman, Sinai and the canal
- **What:** An Ottoman force under Djemal Pasha, which had crossed Sinai, tried to put pontoons across the Suez Canal near Ismailia and was driven off; the canal stayed open.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No Sinai card. `first_battle_of_gaza` mentions Romani, Magdhaba and Rafa as earlier successes, not this 1915 raid. February 1915’s only card is `second_masurian_lakes`.
- **Publishers:**
  - Australian War Memorial, Sinai campaign description: the first Ottoman attack on the canal was on 3 February 1915 and was repulsed.
  - National Army Museum, “Ottoman attack on Suez Canal”: 28 January – 3 February 1915, “the Ottomans fail to capture the Suez Canal”.
- **Hedge:** The crossing attempt is 3 February. The approach fighting is dated from late January (the National Army Museum window opens on 28 January; broader accounts from 26 January). Do not import a casualty figure: the accounts do not agree, and only one of them offers a number.

### `reprisals_order_1915`

- **Date:** 1915-03-11
- **Theatre:** Naval war and the blockade, including neutral trade outside European waters
- **What:** A British Order in Council of 11 March 1915, made as a reprisal for the German submarine war zone, authorised the detention of goods with an enemy destination, origin or ownership, including on ships bound for neutral ports. The Allied governments had announced that intention on 1 March.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `falklands_cruiser_war` narrates the 1914 contraband orders (20 August, 21 September, 29 October). The March 1915 reprisals order, which is the measure that extended the economic war to neutral cargoes, is not a card. Later cards discuss blockade hunger in Germany; they do not date this order.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Blockade”: on 1 March 1915 Britain announced that the Allies would “seize all ships carrying goods of presumed enemy destination, ownership or origin”, and says that this was in effect a blockade but was not legally one.
  - *Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States*, 1916 Supplement: detention of non-contraband goods was enforced by the Order in Council of 11 March 1915, officially titled “Reprisals Restricting German Commerce”. The same order is the subject of a UK National Archives file (“Reprisals Order in Council 11th March 1915”) and of the *European Journal of International Law* (2018), which describes the March 1915 reprisal as a prohibition on neutral ships carrying cargoes to or from Germany, with the word “blockade” avoided.
- **Hedge:** Call it a reprisals order, not a declared blockade. Britannica and the prize-court practice recorded in FRUS both insist on that distinction.

### `twenty_one_demands_signed`

- **Date:** 1915-05-25
- **Theatre:** East Asia, politics
- **What:** China signed the treaties that closed Japan’s Twenty-One Demands, presented on 18 January 1915. Japan had sent an ultimatum on 7 May; China accepted it on 9 May. The agreements confirmed Japan’s claims in Shandong and extended its leases in Manchuria. The demand that Japanese advisers control China’s government was not accepted.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No China or Japan card. May 1915’s cards are Gorlice-Tarnów, the Lusitania (sunk off Ireland) and Italy’s entry.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Twenty-one Demands”: claims dated 18 January 1915; ultimatum on 7 May; Yuan Shikai “capitulated by signing a series of Sino-Japanese agreements on May 25”; he accepted “all but the last point”.
  - Council on Foreign Relations, “China, Japan, and the Twenty-One Demands”: Yuan accepted the ultimatum on 9 May; “the final form of the treaty was signed on May 25, 1915”.

### `south_west_africa_surrenders`

- **Date:** 1915-07-09
- **Theatre:** Africa (German South-West Africa)
- **What:** The German forces in South-West Africa surrendered to General Louis Botha at Khorab, ending the Union of South Africa’s campaign.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `battle_of_tanga` mentions Sandfontein (26 September 1914) in its note and, in the university text, says the colony was “conquered by February 1915”. That sentence is not a card, and February 1915 is when the advance resumed after the Afrikaner rebellion, not the surrender. The National Army Museum dates the renewed advance to late February 1915 and the surrender to 9 July.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum, “German South-West Africa captured” and “South-West African campaign”: the forces surrendered at Khorab on 9 July 1915.
  - South African History Online: on 9 July 1915 the German forces “officially surrendered” to Botha at the Khorab memorial; the instrument was signed by Botha, Governor Seitz and Victor Franke.
- **Hedge:** Do not call the surrender unconditional. South African History Online uses that word; accounts of the instrument describe negotiated terms, including parole for officers. The date and the place are what the two publishers share. Do not add a headcount: the tallies differ.

### `gallipoli_august_offensive`

- **Date:** 1915-08-06
- **Theatre:** Gallipoli
- **What:** British troops landed at Suvla Bay on the evening of 6 August as part of the August offensive, meant to break out of the April beachheads. They consolidated instead of pushing inland, Ottoman reinforcements closed the chance, and a last attack on 21 August failed.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `gallipoli_landings` (25 April) and `gallipoli_evacuation` (19 December) are cards. The August offensive, which decided the campaign, is not. `gallipoli_evacuation` names Suvla only as a beach that was later emptied.
- **Publishers:**
  - Australian War Memorial, “The landing at Suvla Bay”: 6 to 7 August 1915, part of the August offensive; the troops consolidated rather than advancing, and the action at Scimitar Hill and Hill 60 on 21 August failed.
  - Anzac Portal, Department of Veterans’ Affairs, “Landing at Suvla Bay 6 to 15 August 1915”: the landing on the evening of 6 August was part of the August offensive, in support of the attack at Sari Bair.
- **Hedge:** Do not add a casualty figure. The Anzac Portal’s “nearly 20,000” and a War Memorial symposium paper’s wounded count for 6–21 August are not the same series.

### `battle_of_ctesiphon`

- **Date:** 1915-11-22 (window disputed; see hedge)
- **Theatre:** Mesopotamia
- **What:** Townshend’s British-Indian force attacked the Ottoman position at Ctesiphon, short of Baghdad, failed to break it, and retreated down the Tigris towards Kut.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `siege_of_kut` says the advance “was stopped at Ctesiphon” and then narrates the siege from 7 December. The battle is not a card. November 1915’s only card is the Serbian retreat through Albania.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum timeline, “Battle of Ctesiphon”: 22–25 November 1915, “inconclusive”. Its Mesopotamia essay dates the check to 21–23 November and the decision to retreat to Kut.
  - Imperial War Museum, caption for photograph Q 25219: the battle on 23/24 November, then the retirement to Kut. English Wikipedia’s 22–25 November window is the same family as any other Wikipedia edition and is not a second house.
- **Hedge:** The window is disputed (22–25, 21–23, or 23/24 November). Date the card to the 22nd, name the disagreement, and do not add strengths or losses. The retreat to Kut is the point on which the National Army Museum and the Imperial War Museum agree.

### `mora_surrenders`

- **Date:** 1916-02-18
- **Theatre:** Africa (Kamerun)
- **What:** The last German garrison in Kamerun, on the mountain at Mora, capitulated, which the Colonial Office treated as the completion of the Allied conquest.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** Kamerun is absent. February 1916 already has `erzurum_falls`, which is the Caucasus, not West Africa.
- **Publishers:**
  - Hansard, 24 February 1916: Bonar Law told the Commons that a telegram of 18 February reported the capitulation of the Mora garrison, and that “this completes the conquest of the Cameroons”.
  - National Army Museum, “German Kamerun surrenders”: 18 February 1916, “the Allies complete the conquest of German Kamerun”.
- **Hedge:** Charles Dobell’s 1922 account in the *Royal United Services Institution Journal* agrees that Mora surrendered on 18 February after terms were accepted. Do not add the strength of the garrison; the memoirs and the official history do not match.

### `trabzon_occupied`

- **Date:** 1916-04-18
- **Theatre:** Caucasus
- **What:** Russian forces occupied Trabzon (Trebizond) without a fight, after the Ottoman garrison had left the port. It was the largest port taken on the Anatolian Black Sea coast after Erzurum.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `erzurum_falls` (16 February) is the Caucasus card for 1916. Trabzon is not named.
- **Publishers:**
  - *Foreign Relations of the United States*, 1916 Supplement: the US consul at Trebizond, Oscar Heizer, reported that Russian forces occupied the city on Tuesday 18 April 1916 “without opposition by the Turks”, the governor having left on 16 April.
  - *The Times*, 19 April 1916, printing the Petrograd communiqué of 18 April: “Trebizond has been taken”, with the diary line “April 18. Trebizond taken.”
- **Hedge:** A British chronology dates the capture to 17 April. The consul on the spot and the Russian communiqué as printed by *The Times* give 18 April. A Turkish study of the Russian sources likewise puts the Ottoman departure on 16 April and the Russian entry on the morning of the 18th. Use 18 April and name the 17th.

### `battle_of_romani`

- **Date:** 1916-08-04
- **Theatre:** Sinai
- **What:** An Ottoman attack on the railhead at Romani, east of the Suez Canal, was repulsed by British infantry and the Anzac Mounted Division. It was the last Ottoman ground offensive against the canal.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `first_battle_of_gaza` names Romani in one clause as a victory that had already cleared the Sinai. The battle is not a card. August 1916’s cards are Romania’s entry and the Hindenburg–Ludendorff appointment.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum, “Battle of Romani”: 3–5 August 1916, “an Ottoman attack on the British at Romani in the Sinai peninsula fails”. Its Egypt and Palestine essay says the August repulse “marked the end of their campaign against the Suez Canal”.
  - Australian War Memorial, “Battle of Romani”: fought between 3 and 5 August 1916, the main attack early on 4 August; it “finally put a stop to the Turkish threat to the Suez canal”.
- **Hedge:** Date the card 4 August, the day of the main attack, and say the engagement runs 3–5 August. NZ History dates the opening assault to the night of 3 August. Do not copy casualty figures from the War Memorial’s schools page; they are not confirmed by the National Army Museum entry.

### `aqaba_taken`

- **Date:** 1917-07-06
- **Theatre:** Arabia
- **What:** Arab forces, with T. E. Lawrence, took the Ottoman port of Aqaba from the landward side, giving the revolt a Red Sea port close to the Palestine front.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `arab_revolt` is the rising at Mecca on 10 June 1916. Aqaba is not named. July 1917’s cards are the Kerensky offensive, the Reichstag resolution, Corfu and Third Ypres.
- **Publishers:**
  - National Army Museum, “Arab rebels take Aqaba”: 6 July 1917, Arab rebels led by T. E. Lawrence seize the port.
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Al-ʿAqabah”: a fortified Ottoman post, “captured by Arab irregulars led by T. E. Lawrence in July 1917”. The day is from the National Army Museum; the Shapell Manuscript Foundation’s note on a Lawrence letter also dates the capture to 6 July 1917.
- **Hedge:** Do not call the port “heavily fortified” as a measured fact. Britannica says “fortified”; Shapell says “heavily-fortified”; neither is an order of battle.

### `china_declares_war`

- **Date:** 1917-08-14
- **Theatre:** East Asia, politics
- **What:** China declared that a state of war existed with Germany and with Austria-Hungary, and that the treaties with both were abrogated.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No China card. The Chinese Labour Corps appears only as one hypothesis for the origin of the 1918 influenza, inside `influenza_reaches_the_armies`. August 1917’s cards are the Pope’s peace note and Mărășești.
- **Publishers:**
  - *Foreign Relations of the United States*, 1917, Supplement 1: the US minister in Peking forwarded a Chinese Foreign Office note declaring a state of war with Germany and Austria-Hungary from 10 a.m. on 14 August 1917.
  - Library of Congress, “Declarations of War from Around the World”: “On August 14, 1917, the Chinese government declared war on Germany and Austria-Hungary”, citing the Beiyang government gazette of that day. Encyclopaedia Britannica’s war chronology has the same date: “China declares war on Germany and Austria.”
- **Hedge:** The Chinese note in FRUS also says relations with Germany were severed on 14 March 1917. That earlier step was not confirmed here from a second house, so it is not a separate candidate.

### `montagu_declaration`

- **Date:** 1917-08-20
- **Theatre:** India, politics
- **What:** Edwin Montagu told the Commons that British policy was the increasing association of Indians in the administration and “the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire”.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** Indian soldiers are named inside `baghdad_falls`, `first_battle_of_gaza` and `battle_of_megiddo`. There is no card for India’s political place in the war. August 1917 is otherwise European (see `china_declares_war`, which shares the month).
- **Publishers:**
  - *The Spectator*, 25 August 1917: reports Montagu’s statement of the previous Monday, including the “responsible government” formula, and that “substantial steps” would be taken as soon as possible.
  - Hansard, 6 August 1918: Montagu read back “the announcement which I made on behalf of His Majesty’s Government on the 20th of August last year”, with the same formula, and added that substantial steps should be taken as soon as possible.

### `balfour_declaration`

- **Date:** 1917-11-02
- **Theatre:** Palestine, politics
- **What:** Arthur Balfour wrote to Lord Rothschild that the Cabinet viewed with favour “the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people”, with the reservation that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of non-Jewish communities in Palestine, or the rights of Jews in any other country.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** Named, not narrated. `sykes_picot` says the agreement sat beside the Hussein–McMahon letters and the Balfour Declaration. `beersheba_jerusalem` names the Declaration in a clause about promises. Neither card is the letter, and neither is dated 2 November.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Balfour Declaration”: a letter of 2 November 1917 from Balfour to Lionel Walter Rothschild; the national-home sentence; Britannica notes that its meaning is disputed and that it sat in contradiction with Sykes–Picot and the Hussein–McMahon correspondence.
  - Yale Law School, Avalon Project, text of the letter dated 2 November 1917, including both the national-home sentence and the reservation. The Imperial War Museum’s Balfour history page likewise dates the declaration to 1917 and summarises it as British support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine.

### `treaties_of_batum`

- **Date:** 1918-06-04
- **Theatre:** Caucasus, politics
- **What:** At Batum the Ottoman Empire signed treaties with the new republics of Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan, taking territory beyond the line of Brest-Litovsk and a right to move troops across Transcaucasia.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** `battle_of_baku` (26 August) narrates the fight for the city. It does not narrate the June treaties that opened the road for the Ottoman advance. June 1918’s only card is the Piave.
- **Publishers:**
  - Georgian Encyclopedia, “Treaty of Batum”: signed on 4 June 1918 between Georgia and Turkey, and also by the Armenian and Azerbaijani delegations; Batum, Kars, Akhalkalaki and Akhaltsikhe among the districts transferred; the treaty was abolished after Turkey’s defeat.
  - Presidential Library of Azerbaijan: the treaty defines the Turkey–Azerbaijan border and provides for Turkish military assistance to the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic; the treaty with Armenia transferred Echmiadzin and Alexandropol.
- **Hedge:** A Turkish Historical Society article dates the Batum instruments to 4 and 8 June 1918, some of them bilateral and some joint. Use 4 June and say that further instruments were signed over the following days. Do not copy the square-kilometre and population figures; they are one encyclopedia’s arithmetic.

### `armistice_of_mudros`

- **Date:** 1918-10-30
- **Theatre:** Ottoman Empire, politics
- **What:** The Ottoman Empire signed an armistice at Mudros, on Lemnos, with Britain acting for the Allies. It ended the Ottoman war: remaining garrisons outside Anatolia were to be given up, the Straits occupied, and the army demobilised.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** There is no Mudros card. `battle_of_megiddo` and `battle_of_baku` each record the signing of 30 October as a consequence of the fighting. Add this only if the armistice is wanted as its own political event; do not also retell Megiddo.
- **Publishers:**
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Armistice of Mudros”: signed 30 October 1918 at Mudros between the Ottoman Empire and Great Britain representing the Allies; garrisons in the Hejaz, Yemen, Syria, Mesopotamia, Tripolitania and Cyrenaica to be surrendered; the Straits to be occupied; the army demobilised.
  - National Army Museum, “Ottoman Empire signs armistice with Allies”: 30 October 1918, the Armistice of Mudros.

### `lettow_vorbeck_lays_down_arms`

- **Date:** 1918-11-25
- **Theatre:** Africa (East Africa)
- **What:** Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck’s force laid down its arms at Abercorn, in Northern Rhodesia, after a ceasefire earlier in the month. The fighting in East Africa outlasted the 11 November armistice.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** The last card is `armistice_of_11_november_1918`. `battle_of_tanga` says the East African force stayed free for the whole war. `koenigsberg_rufiji` says Lettow-Vorbeck’s campaign “lasted to the armistice”. Neither dates the parade at Abercorn, which is after the armistice and still inside the site’s last month.
- **Publishers:**
  - History of government (UK Foreign Office historians), “The war that did not end at 11am on 11 November”: he formally surrendered his troops at Abercorn on 25 November.
  - The Western Front Association, “November 1918 in East Africa”: on 25 November a formal parade was held at Abercorn and the German weapons were laid down.
- **Hedge:** The day he learned of the armistice is not agreed. The National Army Museum dates the end of fighting to a ceasefire on 14 November. The Western Front Association says he accepted the news on 16 November. The 25 November parade is the shared date. Do not add a headcount: the British, German and South African tallies of Europeans, askari and porters do not match, and Lettow-Vorbeck disputed whether the armistice required surrender or only evacuation.

## Unconfirmed

One publisher only, or the second is not an independent institutional history. Do not write these as cards until a second house is found. No figures are attached.

### `singapore_mutiny` — UNCONFIRMED

- **Date:** 1915-02-15
- **Theatre:** India and colonial manpower
- **What:** Men of the 5th Light Infantry mutinied in Singapore, killed European residents and officers, and were suppressed over the following days with help from local volunteers and from Allied ships in the port.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No card. February 1915 would otherwise have only `suez_canal_raid`, above, and `second_masurian_lakes`.
- **Publisher:** National Library Board, Singapore, Singapore Infopedia, “1915 Singapore Mutiny”: the Right Wing of the 5th Light Infantry revolted on 15 February 1915. An Imperial War Museums “Lives of the First World War” story also places the regiment in Singapore on the morning of 15 February, but that is a contributed life story, not an IWM history article.
- **Hedge:** The Library Board’s own articles do not agree on how many people were killed or how many mutineers were executed. Leave every number out.

### `battle_of_mahiwa` — UNCONFIRMED

- **Date:** 1917-10-15
- **Theatre:** Africa (German East Africa)
- **What:** Lettow-Vorbeck’s force beat a larger British, Nigerian and Indian column at Mahiwa, in the south of the colony, and then had to leave German East Africa for lack of men and ammunition.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** Between `battle_of_tanga` (1914) and the 1918 surrender there is no East African battle. October 1917 already has `beersheba_jerusalem`.
- **Publisher:** National Army Museum timeline, “Battle of Mahiwa”: 15–18 October 1917, a German victory in German East Africa. A second institutional history with the same dates was not found. The Museum’s own East Africa video dates a battle at Mahiwa to July 1917, which contradicts its timeline.
- **Hedge:** The name and the window are also unstable: some British accounts call the fighting the action of Nyangao and date it 16–19 October. Do not use it until a second house picks a window. Do not copy either side’s losses.

### `japan_occupies_north_pacific_islands` — UNCONFIRMED

- **Date:** 1914-10 (month only; no agreed day)
- **Theatre:** Pacific
- **What:** Japan occupied the German islands north of the equator (the Carolines, Marianas and Marshalls), which had been administered from Rabaul but were not included in the Australian capitulation of German New Guinea.
- **Why it is not already in the dump:** No Pacific card separates Japan’s island occupation from Qingdao.
- **Publisher:** Naval Historical Society of Australia, in its New Guinea article: those islands “were occupied in October 1914 by Japan, not Australia”. A second publisher fixing the operation, and any single day, was not found in this pass.
